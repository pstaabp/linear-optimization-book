    Section 6.1 Introduction to Dual Problems

    Objectives

    • Understand the concept of a dual problem in linear optimization.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Be able to write down a dual problem from any primal linear optimization problem.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The concept of a dual problem is one of the most important ideas in linear optimization. It is a way to look at the same problem from a different perspective. The dual problem is related to the primal problem, which is the original problem we are trying to solve. In this section and the next two, we will first determine how to set up a dual problem. In the next section, we will examine the link between the solutions of the primal and dual problems. This will result in a certificate that the solution to the primal problem is optimal.
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.1 Developing a Dual Problem

    We return to Problem 2.1.1, in which Luis is a toymaker who is making decisions about the numbers of toy cars, trucks and SUVs to build to maximize his profit.
    🔗
    The applied problem was written in mathematical form in Section 2.1, but can be summarized as
    🔗

    Problem 6.1.1.

    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \text{Maximize} \amp \amp z = 10 x_1 + 15 x_2 + 12 x_3 \amp \\ \text{subject to} \amp \amp 6 x_1 + 5 x_2 + 4 x_3 \amp \leq 183 \\ \amp \amp 3 x_1 + 6 x_2 + 7 x_3 \amp \leq 204 \\ \amp \amp 45 x_1 + 75 x_2 + 90 x_3 \amp \leq 2655 \\ \text{and} \amp \amp x_1, x_2, x_3 \amp \geq 0. \end{aligned}\tag{6.1.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s pretend we don’t know how to solve this. Instead, we want to see if we can estimate the amount of money that Luis will make. If we take the second constraint and multiply it by 4 to get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 12 x_1 + 24x_2 + 28 x_3 \leq 4(204) = 816 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and use the fact that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = 10 x_1 + 15 x_2 + 12 x_3 \leq 12 x_1 + 24x_2 + 28 x_3 \leq 816. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This shows that the maximize that Luis will earn in the week is less than $816. Also, note that it is important that \(x_1, x_2, x_3 \geq 0\) for the inequalities to hold.
    🔗
    Alternatively, if we take the 3rd equation and divide by 15, we can get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3x_1 + 5x_2 + 6x_3 \leq 177 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And then if we take this equation and add the first two constraints to get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (6x_1+ 5x_2 + 4x_3) + (3x_1 + 6x_2 + 7x_3) + (3x_1 + 5x_2 + 6x_3) = 12 x_1 + 16 x_2 + 17 x_3 \leq 183+204+ 177 = 564 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(z = 10 x_1 + 15 x_2 + 12 x_3 \leq 12 x_1 + 16 x_2 + 17 x_3\text{,}\) we can conclude that \(z \leq 564,\) so this is a better bound than the previous one.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1.2.

    Find another bound for \(z\text{.}\) Can you beat $564? You can take a single inequality and multiply through a constant or take a linear combination like the above example.
    🔗
    🔗
    If we consider any linear combination of the three inequalities, then let \(y_1, y_2\) and \(y_3\) be the arbitrary constants. Then we can write three inequalities in (6.1.1) as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}y_1 (6x_1 + 5x_2 + 4x_3) \leq 183 y_1 \\ y_2 (3x_1 + 6x_2 + 7x_3) \leq 204 y_2 \\ y_3 (45x_1 + 75x_2 + 90x_3) \leq 2655 y_3 \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where it is important to note that \(y_1, y_2, y_3\) must all be nonnegative for the inequalities to hold.
    🔗
    We can then start with objective function \(z\) and set up some cascading inequalities as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} z \amp = 10 x_1 + 15 x_2 + 12 x_3 \\ \amp \geq y_1 (6x_1 + 5x_2 + 4x_3) \\ \amp + y_2 (3x_1 + 6x_2 + 7x_3) \\ \amp + y_3 (45x_1 + 75x_2 + 90x_3) \\ \amp = x_1 (6 y_1 + 3 y_2 + 45 y_3) \\ \amp + x_2 (5 y_1 + 6 y_2 + 75 y_3) \\ \amp + x_3 (4 y_1 + 7 y_2 + 90 y_3 ) \\ \amp \geq 183 y_1 + 204 y_2 + 2655 y_3 \end{aligned}\tag{6.1.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where recall that the first inequality is that we wish to write the objective as a linear combination of existing inequalities, similar to the specific examples above. In the bottom inequality in (6.1.2), since \(x_1, x_2, x_3 \geq 0\text{,}\) the coefficients of \(x_1, x_2, x_3\) must satisfy the inequalities based on the objective function (top line):
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 6 y_1 + 3 y_2 + 45 y_3 \geq 10, \\ 5 y_1 + 6 y_2 + 75 y_3 \geq 15, \\ 4 y_1 + 7 y_2 + 90 y_3 \geq 12. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If we define \(w = 183 x_1 + 204 x_2 + 2655 x_3\text{,}\) then we can determine the smallest bound for \(z\text{,}\) if we minimize \(w\text{.}\) Specifically, we can define the problem
    🔗

    Problem 6.1.3.

    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \text{Minimize} \amp \amp w = 183 x_1 + 204 x_2 + 2655 x_3, \amp \\ \text{subject to} \amp \amp 6 y_1 + 3 y_2 + 45 y_3 \amp \geq 12, \\ \amp \amp 5 y_1 + 6 y_2 + 75 y_3 \amp \geq 15, \\ \amp \amp 4 y_1 + 7 y_2 + 90 y_3 \amp \geq 12, \\ \text{and} \amp \amp y_1, y_2, y_3 \amp \geq 0. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This problem is called the dual of (6.1.1) and we will see in this section that there is an important relationship between a problem and its dual.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.2 Generalizing the Dual

    Recall that in sect ??? we wrote an LOP in standard form as
    🔗

    Problem 6.1.4. Primal Maximal LOP.

    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \text{Maximize} \amp \amp z = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j x_j \amp \\ \text{subject to} \amp \amp \sum_{j=1}^n a_{i,j} x_j \amp \leq b_j \qquad \text{for $1 \leq i \leq m$} \\ \text{and} \amp \amp x_j \amp \geq 0, \qquad \text{for $1 \leq j \leq n$} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    is labeled Primal to distinguish it from the dual and a maximal problem. This can be written in matrix form as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \text{Maximize} \amp \amp z = \boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{x} \\ \text{subject to} \amp \amp A \boldsymbol{x} \leq \boldsymbol{b} \\ \text{and} \amp \amp \boldsymbol{x} \geq 0. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The dual of this problem can be written as
    🔗

    Problem 6.1.5. Dual Minimal LOP.

    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \text{Minimize} \amp \amp w = \sum_{i=1}^n b_i y_i \amp \\ \text{subject to} \amp \amp \sum_{i=1}^n a_{i,j} y_i \amp \geq c_i \qquad \text{for $1 \leq j \leq n$} \\ \text{and} \amp \amp y_i \amp \geq 0, \qquad \text{for $1 \leq i \leq m$} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    or in matrix form as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \text{Minimize} \amp \amp w = \boldsymbol{b}^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{y} \\ \text{subject to} \amp \amp A^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{y} \geq \boldsymbol{c} \\ \text{and} \amp \amp \boldsymbol{y} \geq 0. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1.6.

    Write down the dual of Problem 2.2.2 and then find its optimal solution using the simplex method finding the final tableau.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The dual can be written as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \text{Minimize} \amp \amp w = 120 y_1 + 40 y_2 + 16 y_3 \amp \\ \text{subject to} \amp \amp 4y_1 + 2 y_2 \amp \geq 1 \\ \amp \amp 3y_1 + 2 y_2 + y_3 \amp \geq 3 \\ \text{and} \amp \amp y_1, y_2, y_3 \amp \geq 0, \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The solution can be found by starting with the tableau:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{rrr|rrr|r} -4 \amp -2 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp -3\\ -3 \amp -2 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp -3\\ \hline 120 \amp 40 \amp 16 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0\\ \end{array} \right] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and then performing the simplex method to get the final tableau:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{r} 1 \mapsto 4, \\ 2 \mapsto 5, \\ 5 \mapsto 1 \end{array} \qquad \left[\begin{array}{rrr|rrr|r} 2 \amp 0 \amp -2 \amp -2 \amp 2 \amp 0 \amp 0\\ 4 \amp 2 \amp 0 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 3\\ \hline 80 \amp 0 \amp 32 \amp 40 \amp 0 \amp 2 \amp -120\\ \end{array} \right] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This has the solution
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y} = \frac{1}{2} \left[\begin{array}{rrr|rr} 0 \amp 3 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array}\right]. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.3 Weak Primal-Dual Theorem

    A generalization of the inequalities in (6.1.2) is the following.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} z = \boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{x} \leq (A^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{y})^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{y}^{\intercal} A \boldsymbol{x} \leq \boldsymbol{y}^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{b} = w\tag{6.1.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where the first inequality arises from the Dual problem, the equality in the middle is a result of the property of transposes of matrices and the last inequality is from the Primal problem..
    🔗
    If we examine the inequalities in (6.1.3), this is true for any \(\boldsymbol{x}\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}\) that satisfy the constraints in the primal and dual problems, respectively. In short, \(\boldsymbol{x}\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}\) must be feasible points.
    🔗
    One of the results of (6.1.3) is the relationship
    🔗

    Theorem 6.1.7. Weak Duality Theorem.

    \begin{equation*} z = \boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{x} \leq \boldsymbol{b}^{\intercal} \boldsymbol{y} = w \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for any point \(\boldsymbol{x}\) that is feasible in the Primal problem and \(\boldsymbol{y}\) a feasible point in the Dual problem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    This is a direct result of the inequalities in (6.1.3).
    🔗
    🔗
    As an example, in Subsection 6.1.1, some attempts to find a bound on the maximum profit from Luis the toymaker. For example, the first bound of $816 was found by taking the second constraint and multiplying it by 4, which is a feasible point in the dual problem. The second bound of $564 was found by taking a linear combination of the first two constraints. The first bound is actually \(\boldsymbol{y} = [0~4~0]^{\intercal}\) and the second bound is \(\boldsymbol{y} = [1~1~\frac{1}{15}]^{\intercal}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The bounds can be calculated by using the objective function in the dual problem in Problem 6.1.3. For the first bound:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} w = 183 \cdot 0 + 204 \cdot 4 + 2655 \cdot 0 = 816. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and for the second bound:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} w = 183 \cdot 1 + 204 \cdot 1 + 2655 \cdot \frac{1}{15} = 564. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1.8.

    For these two bounds, show that the value of \(\boldsymbol{y}\) are feasible points in the dual problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For the first bound, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 6 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 4 + 45 \cdot 0 \amp \geq 12 \\ 5 \cdot 0 + 6 \cdot 4 + 75 \cdot 0 \amp \geq 15 \\ 4 \cdot 0 + 7 \cdot 4 + 90 \cdot 0 \amp \geq 12. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and for the second bound, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 6 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 1 + 45 \cdot \frac{1}{15} \amp = 6 + 3 + 3 = 12 \geq 12 \\ 5 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 1 + 75 \cdot \frac{1}{15} \amp = 5 + 6 + 5 = 16 \geq 15 \\ 4 \cdot 1 + 7 \cdot 1 + 90 \cdot \frac{1}{15} \amp = 4 + 7 + 6 = 17 \geq 12. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
